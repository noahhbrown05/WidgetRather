01 — The thing you asked about

An app is two halves

Every app is split in two, and the split is what "front end" and "back end" are naming. Amazon's own explainer puts it like this:

"You can think of the frontend as the exterior of a car and the backend as all the machinery inside. A beautifully designed car will only run optimally if the internal machinery works properly." Amazon Web Services, Front End vs Back End

Noah's half

Front end

"The frontend is what your users see and includes visual elements like buttons, checkboxes, graphics, and text messages."

In Widget Rather: every screen, the widget's look, the Pastel Critters, the pink and blue option pills, the results bars, the emoji reaction row. It lives on each person's phone.

Greg's half

Back end

"The backend is the data and infrastructure that make your application work. It stores and processes application data."

In Widget Rather: the question schedule, everybody's votes, the group percentages, and the rules about who's allowed to see what. It lives on one computer that all the phones talk to.

The test that always works

If it happens on your phone, it's front end. If it happens on one shared computer that every phone talks to, it's back end. That's it. That's the whole distinction.

AWS points out the consequence that actually matters: on the front end "every user has their own copy of an application in their browser or mobile app", while "the backend may have to handle thousands of requests simultaneously." One copy each, versus one copy for everyone.

So why do we need a back end at all?

Because Widget Rather is about other people. A calculator app needs no back end — everything happens on your phone and nobody else is involved. But "how did my friends answer?" is information that is not on your phone. Something has to hold everyone's answers in one place and do the counting. That something is the back end.

The back end exists because the app is social. If we ever cut the social part, most of Greg's job disappears with it.

02 — The mechanism

One morning, start to finish

This is the entire product in seven steps. Follow the numbers and watch where the line gets crossed — and, at step 4, where it doesn't.

Step 4 is the thing that just changed

We assumed a widget button could talk straight to the server. Expo's own documentation says it can't: widget code "cannot perform asynchronous work, import other modules, or access your app's runtime or in-memory state", and the value a button's onPress returns just "becomes the widget's new props."

Translated: tapping the widget writes to your own phone, and the vote only reaches everyone else the next time you open the app. The widget can flip to "answered" instantly, but any numbers it shows are from the last push, not from your tap. That's what decision D-020 is about, and it's why the spec's core loop needs one sentence reworded.

Noah — this is the shortest path forward

Three boxes to tick in DECISIONS.md. In plain terms:

D-018

Public communities show numbers only. You'd see the split, the insights and how many people are in it — but no list of members, no individual strangers' picks, no reactions between strangers. Cheapest to build, and much less to moderate.

D-019

Cap a friend group at 50 people. The spec currently says no cap. "Your twin" compares you against every other member, so an uncapped group gets expensive fast. Fifty is big enough that no real friend group hits it.

D-020

A widget tap is saved on your phone and syncs later. Step 4 of the diagram. Not a choice we get to make — it's what Expo's documentation says a widget can do — but the spec's wording needs to match reality.

Why it matters: D-018 and D-019 are the conditions Greg attached to signing the MVP scope. If either goes the other way, D-008 drops back to PROPOSED and stage 1 isn't finished.

05 — What building actually looks like

How one feature gets made

When the hold lifts, every feature gets built the same four ways round. It's the order Chris Raroque uses, and the reason it works is that Noah never has to wait for Greg to start.

STEP 1

Screen with fake data

Noah builds the results screen with made-up numbers typed straight into the code. It looks finished. It's connected to nothing.

STEP 2

Real data behind it

Greg builds the tables and writes getGroupResults(), which returns numbers in exactly the shape Noah's fake ones had.

STEP 3

Swap one for the other

Noah deletes the fake numbers and calls Greg's function instead. If step 1 and step 2 agreed on the shape, this is nearly a one-line change.

STEP 4

Polish and edge cases

What if you're offline? What if the group is empty? What if nobody's voted yet? This part takes longer than you'd think.

Then: one branch, one pull request, the other person reads it, merge. Raroque's own summary of the sequence is "UI with dummy data → data model/backend → connect → polish" (Q13QOgwoF0E @ 01:02, via raroque-playbook.md).

The one habit worth stealing now

Agree the shape of the data before either of you starts. If Noah's fake results and Greg's real results have the same field names, connecting them is trivial. If they don't, it's a day of arguing. That's the entire reason the "contract" rule exists in MVP-GAMEPLAN.md.
